    1. Use four mandatory lenses: People, Opportunity, Context, and Risk and Reward.
   Every plan must address all four and explain their connections. For example, a growth strategy must match the team’s capabilities, available capital, and market conditions. Merely giving each topic a heading is insufficient.
    2. Establish the purpose of the plan before drafting.
    Identify the business stage, intended reader, geography, and decision the plan should support. Adapt the depth and financing discussion accordingly. Do not automatically impose venture-capital expectations on a small, independently owned business.
    3. Separate evidence, assumptions, and unknowns.
    Label material claims as supported facts, estimates, assumptions, or unresolved questions. Cite sources for external claims, including their dates and relevant geography. Never invent interviews, customer demand, traction, partnerships, credentials, or financial results. When information is missing, state what evidence is needed.
    4. Assess the people through evidence of execution.
    Explain the founders’ relevant experience, accomplishments, skills, industry relationships, credibility, and history of working together. Address commitment, motivations, judgment under pressure, and willingness to make difficult decisions. Include essential outside contributors, such as suppliers and advisers. Identify missing capabilities and a realistic plan to obtain them.
    5. Define the customer and the reason to buy.
    Identify the initial customer segment, its problem, existing alternatives, and the benefit offered. Distinguish users, buyers, and purchasing decision-makers where relevant. Explain the buying process, urgency, willingness to pay, and barriers to adoption. Treat enthusiasm or expressed interest as weaker evidence than actual purchasing behavior.
    6. Evaluate market size and industry attractiveness separately.
    Explain whether the relevant market is large, growing, or sufficient for the business’s goals. Then examine whether its structure permits sustainable profits: competitive intensity, customer and supplier power, capital requirements, product life cycles, and ongoing investment needs. A large market alone does not establish an attractive opportunity. If the industry is difficult, explain why this venture can still succeed.
    7. Make customer acquisition and retention explicit.
    Describe how the business will reach its initial customers and subsequent segments. Account for acquisition time, sales effort, channel costs, delivery costs, customer support, and retention. Explain why each channel is economically viable. Do not assume that launching a good product will automatically produce customers.
    8. Justify pricing through customer value and business economics.
    Explain what customers receive, what alternatives cost, and what supports the proposed price. Show whether that price covers production, delivery, support, and acquisition costs while contributing to overhead and profit. Identify how willingness to pay will be tested. Do not assume either premium pricing or low pricing is inherently the correct strategy.
    9. Explain how the product or service will actually be delivered.
    Describe the path from development to launch and ongoing operations. Identify essential resources, suppliers, capabilities, dependencies, lead times, and capacity constraints. Include the commercial work needed to turn an invention into a functioning business.
    10. Build financial projections from the business’s operating drivers.
        Identify the few variables that determine success—for example, conversion, repeat purchases, capacity utilization, production yield, or distribution costs. Make revenue and cost forecasts traceable to these variables. Match forecast detail to available evidence; avoid precise-looking long-term numbers with unsupported assumptions. Place detailed financial schedules after the core business explanation.
    11. Answer profitability and cash-flow questions separately.
        State the sales level required to break even and the conditions under which operating cash flow becomes positive. Explain when resources must be obtained, when suppliers and employees must be paid, how long customer acquisition takes, and when customer payments arrive. Include equipment and working-capital requirements. Show the maximum funding gap and distinguish operating cash generation from cash received through financing.
    12. Analyze competitors as organizations that will react.
        Cover direct competitors, substitutes, and the customer’s option to do nothing. Assess competitors’ resources, advantages, and weaknesses. Explain their likely response to market entry and how the venture could respond in turn. Consider future entrants and potential alliances. Reject unsupported claims of “no competition” or a permanently unbeatable position.
    13. Explain how advantages can endure.
        Identify what makes the business difficult to copy or displace and what could weaken that advantage. If the opportunity depends on a temporary price difference, subsidy, regulatory gap, or other short-lived condition, explain how the business will become sustainable before that condition disappears.
    14. Make growth a sequence of supported choices.
        Explain which products, customer segments, or regions could follow the initial business and why existing capabilities provide an advantage in reaching them. State the additional people, capital, distribution, and operational capacity required. Distinguish the committed launch plan from possible future expansion.
    15. Connect external conditions to specific business consequences.
        Assess relevant regulation, technology, demographics, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, and other external forces. For each material factor, explain how it affects demand, costs, operations, funding, or timing. Describe plausible changes, management’s response, and any realistic ability to influence those conditions. Avoid a generic list of trends.
    16. Pair each major risk with a response.
        Address risks involving people, customers, competitors, suppliers, technology, operations, cash, and regulation as applicable. For each significant risk, specify the warning sign, likely consequence, mitigation, contingency action, and responsible person. State what remains exposed after mitigation. Do not present risk transfer as making risk disappear.
    17. Show more than one plausible future.
        Include downside, base, and upside cases driven by changes in identifiable assumptions. Explain the consequences for cash needs, timing, viability, and returns. Test which assumptions matter most. Use probability estimates only when justified; do not invent confidence to make scenarios look scientific.
    18. Treat the venture as a series of experiments.
        Identify the assumptions whose failure would most threaten the business and test them early. For each proposed experiment, specify the hypothesis, method, cost, duration, success threshold, and resulting decision. Use prototypes, pilots, pricing tests, or limited launches where appropriate. Explain when evidence would justify continuing, changing direction, or stopping.
    19. Tie funding to milestones and investor fit.
        Explain how much capital is needed, when it is needed, and what it will establish or achieve. Account for delays and disappointing results. Evaluate prospective investors’ expertise, relationships, expectations, and behavior during difficulty alongside valuation and dilution. Favor understandable arrangements with aligned incentives that remain workable when events differ from the plan.
    20. Explain how financial value can ultimately be realized.
        Where external investment is relevant, describe credible routes to returns, such as distributions or acquisition; consider a public listing only when appropriate. Address modest outcomes and orderly wind-down as well as success. Explain how partnerships and financing choices might preserve or restrict future options.
    21. Keep the plan open to revision.
        Define milestones, metrics, review points, and triggers for changing the plan. Record what new evidence changes and why. Update connected assumptions consistently: a slower sales cycle must also affect revenue timing, staffing, cash requirements, and funding needs.
    22. Use the reference critically.
        Preserve its analytical principles while checking current facts independently. Treat the article’s 1997 industry examples, investor thresholds, and broad statements about capital availability as historical context. Do not convert its rhetorical remarks about risk, contracts, or investor behavior into universal rules.
    23. Write with candor and decision-making value.
        Explain weaknesses, contradictory evidence, and potentially fatal flaws directly. Replace promotional adjectives with evidence and reasoning. Every major section should establish a conclusion, its supporting evidence, its uncertainty, and its implication for action.
    24. Apply a completion check before calling the plan ready.
        Confirm that all four lenses are covered; customer access and pricing are credible; profitability and cash timing are distinct; forecasts match operational capacity; major risks have responses; and funding connects to milestones. Mark unresolved items explicitly. A complete draft may contain unknowns, but it must make clear which ones prevent a confident launch or funding decision.
